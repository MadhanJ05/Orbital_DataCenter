The catch

Earth runs out of power before it runs out of money

58%

served on Earth alone

We ran our model with Earth sites only, using every trick we found. The 500 MW grid limit is full when 58% of people are served. The other 42% need computers that bring their own power.

So we add computers in orbit. Up there, solar panels see the Sun almost all the time, and there is no grid limit.

Our orbits

Riding the line between day and night

All satellites fly dawn-dusk sun-synchronous orbits: the ring follows the day/night line and turns about 1° a day, so it keeps the same angle to the Sun all year.

  • Heightabout 650 km
  • Tilt to the equator98° (passes near both poles)
  • One lap98 min · 44,160 km · 7.5 km/s
  • Round trip to users40 ms
  • Radiation over 5 years0.75 krad
Orbit and radiation dose as in Google's Suncatcher study · tilt and lap from Kepler's law and Earth's bulge · round trip: slide 12

  • Published shadow-free orbit~1,600 km

Before we fixed it

Our first design bunched everything into a few big places. Losing its orbit section left 37% of live users unserved and background jobs waiting over 32 hours. Spreading over 12 planes plus backup costs about $3.0B, and is why Design 1 is the safe one.

What we learned

Three surprises

01

Earth runs out of power before money

Unlimited solar power isn't the same as cheap power: orbit only matches the best Earth sites once satellites get much lighter.

02

How you use chips beats where they sit

Chips are two-thirds of the cost on Earth and in orbit. Letting background agents work in quiet hours saved $4.8B.

03

Some smart-sounding ideas don't pay

Running chips hotter to shrink radiators, and sending easy questions to a smaller AI, both made things worse.
