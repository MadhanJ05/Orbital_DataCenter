Sundai Hack 145 · Track 1

How we serve 4.45 billion AI users, with help from space

A plain-language guide to our two designs and the tests we ran on them. No space or chip knowledge needed.

The job

Build the computers behind everyone's AI in 2035

Picture a giant kitchen that has to cook every AI answer in the world: chat replies for 4 billion people, plus AI "agents" that do hours of work for 450 million developers and office workers. The organizers gave us four rules.

$30Bto build and run it for 5 years
500 MWof Earth's power grid, in total, for all our sites
$200/kgto launch anything into space (sunlight up there is free)
0.6 s · 2 sfor chat and for AI agents to start answering
The catch

Earth runs out of power first

Money is not the first problem. Electricity is. Even with every trick we found, Earth's 500 MW is only enough for a bit more than half of everyone.

Our answer

Two designs that both meet every rule

Both serve 100% of the 4.45 billion users. They differ in how much sits on Earth and how heavy each satellite is.

Design 1 · the safe one

Earth and orbit, working together

  • On Earth6 sites, 323 MW
  • In orbit12 groups × 50 MW (84 satellites)
  • Satellite weight18 t per MW
  • Launched11,661 t
  • 5-year cost$29.88B

Uses the organizers' numbers exactly. Cheap, clean Earth power where it exists, orbit for the rest.

Design 2 · our big idea

Everything in orbit, with a lighter satellite

  • On Earthnone
  • In orbit12 groups × 75 MW (120 satellites)
  • Satellite weight11.0 t per MW
  • Launched11,452 t
  • 5-year cost$29.44B

Our "what if": a lighter satellite makes space cheaper than the best place on Earth, so the whole fleet can leave the grid.

A common question

How much do we launch?

Think of delivery trucks. Design 2 sends 50% more computing to orbit, because it moved the whole job off Earth, but each of its trucks is 40% lighter. So both designs launch about the same weight.

Design 2 carries more batteries (on every rack, not just the live ones), and 5% of chips in space break with nobody to fix them, so both designs launch a little extra.

How it fits the budget

The tricks that made it work

  1. Off-peak shift. Background agents get 4 hours to finish. They use the racks that serve live users during quiet hours, so we buy fewer racks. Saves about $4.8B.
  2. Small helpers. Half of each agent's steps go to a smaller AI model that is about 10× cheaper per step.
  3. Guess ahead. The AI drafts a few words at once and checks them together, so each chip writes 2–3× more.
  4. The right chip for each job. One kind of chip reads the question, another writes the answer.
  5. Many small places instead of one big one. 6 Earth sites and 12 satellite groups, so one outage only takes out a small slice.
  6. Pause idle tool sandboxes. A background agent's tool computer is saved to disk while the AI is writing, and woken up when its tool runs, so we buy fewer of them.
Test 1 · the 24-hour simulation

We played out a full day, minute by minute

The off-peak shift is our biggest saving, so we had to prove it works. The simulation rebuilds a real day: people in the Americas, Europe/Africa and Asia each get busy in their own afternoon. Someone is always awake, but most people live in Asia and almost nobody over the Pacific, so the whole world is still below its average demand 13 hours a day. Every minute the simulation does three things.

  1. Serve every live user first (chat and live agents). Nobody waits.
  2. Give the leftover racks to background agent jobs.
  3. Put any job that doesn't fit in a waiting line, and time how long it waits. It must finish within its 4-hour deadline.
Test 2 · switch things off

What if a site or satellite group goes dark?

We re-ran the day 18 times for Design 1, each time with one place switched off for the whole day. Every time, all live users were still served and background jobs waited under 1.5 hours. We also ran the worst eclipse day for each half of the satellite groups, alone and together with every possible outage: still no live user unserved, and the longest background wait was 200 minutes, inside the limit.

Before we fixed it

Our first design had everything bunched into a few big places. Losing its orbit section left 37% of live users without service and made background jobs wait over 32 hours. Spreading out over 12 satellite groups and adding backup costs about $3.0B, and it is why Design 1 is the safe one.

Test 3 · what if our guesses are wrong?

We made each of our guesses 25% worse

The organizers didn't give every number, like local electricity prices, so we had to guess some. We pushed each guess 25% the wrong way, one at a time. Both designs also keep 5% of every chip's memory free, as real systems do.
