Build the computers behind everyone's AI in 2035

The catch

Earth runs out of power first

Money is not the first problem. Electricity is. We ran our model with Earth sites only, using every trick we found: the 500 MW grid limit is full when 58% of people are served.

Architecture · the big picture

How a question travels

Every question goes to the nearest place that has free capacity. In Design 1 that can be one of six data centers on Earth, or a satellite. In Design 2 it is always a satellite. Satellites talk to the ground with lasers through 16 ground stations spread around the world.

Our orbits

Where the satellites fly

All our satellites fly in dawn-dusk sun-synchronous orbits. In plain words: the ring of satellites rides along the line between day and night, and it turns a little each day to keep the same angle to the Sun all year. So the satellites see sunlight almost all the time.

Why 650 km and not higher

HeightEclipses?Longest eclipseExtra one-way delay
650 km (ours)about 95 days a year19.6 min2.2 ms
1,000 kmyes12.9 min3.3 ms
1,300 kmyes5.5 min4.3 ms
1,400 kmnevernone4.7 ms

Above 1,400 km there are no eclipses, but that is inside the Van Allen radiation belt, it costs more to reach, and dead satellites stay up for centuries instead of years. Batteries for our eclipses cost only $0.06B (Design 1) or $0.11B (Design 2). A tilted orbit like Starlink's would be in shadow for about 35 of every 95 minutes, on almost every lap, so every rack would need much bigger batteries.

How it fits the budget

The tricks that made it work

  1. Off-peak shift. Background agents get 4 hours to finish. They use the live-agent racks during quiet hours, so we buy fewer racks. Saves about $4.8B.
  2. Small helpers. Half of each agent's steps go to a smaller AI model that is about 10× cheaper per step.
  3. Guess ahead. The AI drafts a few words at once and checks them together, so each chip writes 2–3× more.
  4. The right chip for each job. One kind of chip reads the question, another writes the answer.
  5. Many small places instead of a few big ones. 6 Earth sites and 12 planes, so one outage takes out only a small slice, and the backup we keep is small.
  6. Pause idle tool sandboxes. An agent's sandbox is saved to disk while the AI is writing, and woken up when its tool runs, so we buy fewer of them.

Test 4 · the 24-hour simulation

We played out a full day, minute by minute

The off-peak shift is our biggest saving, so we had to prove it works. The simulation rebuilds a real day: people in the Americas, Europe/Africa and Asia each get busy in their own afternoon. Someone is always awake, but most people live in Asia and almost nobody over the Pacific, so world demand is below its average 13 hours a day. That matches the organizers' figure that the busiest hour is 2.5× the average slide 16. Every minute the simulation does three things:

  1. Serve every live user first (chat and live agents). Nobody waits.
  2. Give the leftover racks to background agent jobs.
  3. Put any job that doesn't fit in a waiting line, and time how long it waits. It must finish within its 4-hour deadline.

Before we fixed it

Our first design had everything bunched into a few big places. Losing its orbit section left 37% of live users without service and made background jobs wait over 32 hours. Spreading out over 12 planes and adding backup costs about $3.0B, and it is why Design 1 is the safe one.

What we learned

Three surprises

  1. Earth runs out of power before it runs out of money. Unlimited solar power isn't the same as cheap power: orbit only matches the best Earth sites once satellites get much lighter.
  2. How you use chips matters more than where they sit. Chips are about two-thirds of the cost on Earth and in orbit. Letting background agents work in quiet hours saved $4.8B.
  3. Some smart-sounding ideas don't pay. Running chips hotter to shrink radiators, or sending easy questions to a smaller AI, both made things worse.
